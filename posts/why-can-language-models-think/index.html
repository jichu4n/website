Why Can Language Models Think?

November 24, 2026
Essays

AI systems powered by language models are increasingly performing many practical tasks previously only achievable with human cognition. There’s much to be discussed about the implications, but in this post I want to focus on the why.

When the first LLM systems appeared, it was not obvious that they would eventually evolve human-like cognitive capabilities. As many skeptics pointed out, LLMs are fundamentally stochastic text generators. Our brains are vastly more complex. We evolved through millions of years of evolutionary pressure, and go through years of education to build professional competency in the modern world. So how come we are suddenly worried about being replaced by next-token predictors within the span of a few years?

I believe the key lies in natural language.

Natural language is a model of human cognition. A computational system over natural language can, with sufficient computational power and prior knowledge, simulate human cognition to a level sufficient for many practical applications.

Natural languages are models of human cognition

Consider two natural languages like English and Japanese. There are obvious differences you’d notice immediately, like the words, sounds and writing systems. But the differences go deeper: the two languages model the same ideas in different ways, not just in how they encode information, but in what information they encode. In effect, each language provides a different abstraction model for our ideas. If you examine the same conversation in idiomatic English and Japanese, you’d be struck by the difference between what information is actually transmitted, even while being similarly effective at conveying the same ideas in practice.

For example:

English:

  • Q: Did you eat the apple?
  • A: Yeah, I did.

Japanese:

  • Q: リンゴ、食べた？ → Apple(s), ate (casual register past tense)
  • A: うん、食べたよ。→ Yeah, ate (casual register past tense) [assertive marker].

Even from this very brief example, we can see major differences in how these languages work:

  • In the English question, the phrase “the apple” indicates we’re talking about a single, specific apple the two people have shared context on. The Japanese version does not include this information - we could be talking about a single specific apple, or multiple apples, or apples in general.
  • In the Japanese version, the casual register explicitly indicates these speakers have a close relationship, for example friends or family members. In the English version, the social context is much more ambiguous.
  • The English question and answer both indicate who performed the act of eating (”you” and “I” respectively). The Japanese versions do not include this information. In fact, from the text alone, we can’t fully rule out they’re talking about a different third person rather than the answerer.
  • The Japanese answer emphasizes affirmation with multiple signals. It repeats the original verb (”食べた”), contains one affirmative marker at the start (うん) and an assertive marker at the end (よ). In comparison, the English answer is less emphatic; it omits the original verb (”ate”) in favor of emphasizing past tense (”did”), and only has a single affirmative marker (”yeah”).

This leads to an obvious question - if our natural languages have such different abstraction models, why are we able to translate between them at all?

If you’re multilingual, the answer might feel obvious. Despite their differences, modern natural languages are remarkably similar in expressive power. The words, sounds and grammar may be wildly different. Ideas that are succinctly conveyed in one language might require additional clarification in another. Some nuances may be lost in translation, because the extra verbosity needed to bring them along isn't worth it. But it is still possible to convey the same ideas in different languages, just via different abstractions.

There’s a parallel here with programming languages. If you look at languages like C and Python, they look very different in terms of syntax, practical capabilities, and the abstraction models they provide. But fundamentally, they have the same level of expressive power as defined by the computational space of the Turing Machine. As a result, assuming a corresponding compiler and runtime, it is always possible to translate any C program to Python or vice versa, even if the resulting programs would look very different next to each other. This is the fundamental reason why programming languages with very different abstractions can still run on the same CPU or bytecode runtime.

Similarly, natural languages have similar expressive power despite their enormous differences because they represent different abstraction models over the common computational space of human cognition. Natural languages are not random accidents, even if they might appear arbitrary on the surface. They are systems that have been continuously optimized over hundreds of thousands of years to better model our ideas. So it only makes sense that natural languages would converge over time towards a common ceiling of expressive power, defined by the extent of our cognitive needs and abilities.

We say “a picture is worth a thousand words”, and indeed a large portion of our daily cognitive activity is non-verbal. But consider how much of our knowledge and skill is acquired and applied through the medium of language, spoken or written. Our cognition is not limited to language, but language is perhaps the most powerful abstraction model of our cognition.

Simulating cognition through natural language

However, languages are still abstraction models, not full descriptions. To use an analogy, a Python program does not explicitly describe memory allocation and operating system APIs. To run it, we need a Python interpreter that implements those abstractions. In the same way, simulating cognition through natural language requires a runtime that is capable of interpreting its abstraction model.

For example, we might naturally say something in conversation like “Apple dropped 6% yesterday”. But consider how much we’re assuming the listener already knows: Apple the company, the stock market and how it works, the concept of time and date, an intuition around numbers and scale, and the ability to logically deduce we’re not talking about a literal fruit dropping to the ground. Without the necessary shared context and cognitive primitives, the idea being conveyed cannot be understood from the words alone.

Today, we see empirically that the threshold has been crossed. Current LLM-based AI systems are runtimes for natural language sufficiently powerful to solve many practical knowledge tasks.

First - algorithms. Many valuable cognitive tasks are algorithms that can be described and applied in natural language. We know this because this is how humans can learn and perform the same tasks - for example, consider how an engineer might learn to debug production issues through an oncall playbook written by other engineers. A sufficiently powerful computational system can achieve the same result by computing the equivalent steps in natural language. This does not necessarily match how humans perform these tasks in practice; with training, our brains may be able to build specialized neural circuitry to execute these tasks more efficiently without using language. But the fact that the algorithms and solutions can be described via language makes it possible for a sufficiently capable system to compute them through language.

Second - data. Today’s LLMs have broad knowledge through training and tools. Frontier LLMs are trained on a vast corpus of accumulated human knowledge, and are often provided tools for retrieving additional context. So when it sees the word “apple”, the model already has a significant amount of knowledge about apples, Apple the company, and related concepts associated with either. It can decide to look up additional information it doesn’t already have in its training data, such as the latest news about Apple the company. This makes it possible for a model to interpret natural language with a human-like (or rather, super-human-like) level of shared context.

Is there a ceiling?

Computers have outperformed humans at narrow intellectual tasks for decades. CPUs perform arithmetic orders of magnitude faster than any person, and specialized AI systems mastered chess and Go years ago. But excellence in an isolated domain never implied general cognitive capability. Today’s LLMs are the first computational systems to empirically demonstrate that computation over natural language can simulate human cognition to a level sufficient for many practical applications.

The scary thing is that, following from our framework, there isn’t necessarily a theoretical ceiling for the cognitive ability of similar computational systems. LLMs may not be able to perform a specific cognitive task today. But as long as the task can be described by natural language, there’s no reason why a better LLM, or a different computational architecture based on language, cannot do so in the future.

The implications are profound.

Software engineering is the clearest early indicator of the societal impact. In the span of a year, the software industry has been completely upended - after half a century, writing code by hand is suddenly becoming the exception at many companies. Today, coding agents can translate requirements into implementations, run test suites, debug failures, and iterate toward working solutions increasingly autonomously.

But nothing about LLMs’ ability to solve cognitive tasks is specific to software engineering. It would be naive to not expect the same transformations to happen across financial analysis, law, scientific research, and other knowledge work industries, though they will likely move at different speeds.

Ultimately, I don’t subscribe to a pessimistic outlook. The ability to solve human-level cognitive tasks through natural language is genuinely a step-function change in our technological arc as a species. Throughout history, every such step-function change has come with challenges in the short term, and yet led to beneficial outcomes in the long term. I believe future generations will live in a transformed, but ultimately better world.
